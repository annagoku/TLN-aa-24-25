CHAPTER 1: Behaviour and Sense Organs

Understanding your horse is essential for anyone who wants to experience life in close contact with a truly unique animal.
To understand it means to know its ancient nature.
Like every mammal, the horse acquires its sensory perceptions through organs that are differently developed from ours: more receptive and sensitive apparatuses that analyze signals, decoding them into an ancient language, typical of a herd herbivore, prey to hunters, whose main defense is flight.

Through the sense of smell, the horse perceives much of the surrounding world; one reason for the length of the horse's head is likely the need to host a very large area of olfactory mucosa, allowing it to analyse and recognize many scents.

Horses see quite well even at night.
Their eyes are larger than those of whales and elephants. The large eye, with a greater number of receptive cells, enhances both visual sharpness and field of vision.
An important characteristic of the horse is the position of its eyes on the sides of its head.
This means that the horse has a very wide field of vision, but also that its binocular vision—where both eyes see together—is relatively small.

The ears are the organs of hearing, whose outer parts are highly mobile, move independently in any direction, and are particularly well-suited to capturing sounds.
In horses, the ears also have a secondary function as a means of communication.

Horses use touch to strengthen bonds between individuals, highlighting how important it is for the horse to be touched.
The sense of touch is especially crucial for gathering information about the surrounding environment—particularly the nose and nostrils, which are equipped with long hairs used to examine the placement and texture of objects.

The horse is, therefore, a very sensitive animal, ready to detect any sign of danger.
In the relationship that humans establish with it, they must use communication that is understandable, calming, and made of small gestures executed with great calm and determination.

Horses communicate with their herd companions by sniffing, neighing, raising a foreleg, licking or pressing their muzzles against other horses’ bodies, or by bucking and kicking, usually without intent to harm.
When a horse curls its upper lip, it is called the flehmen response, which occurs in reaction to strong odors or tastes, such as when stallions sense a mare in heat.

When things around them become interesting, horses become alert and ready to move—flight is their defense and solution to many problems.
The resulting muscle tension produces a posture characterized by a high carriage of the head and tail.
Other horses, seeing a companion assume this posture, will adopt it as well, warned of something they have not yet perceived.

To signal irritation, horses use gestures that typically serve to drive off annoying elements from their bodies, such as flies.
Head movements, tail swishing, and pawing the ground with front or hind legs are signals that usually indicate minor discomfort.

In nature, horses are gregarious and social animals, for the ancient reason of facing predators as a group.
Stallions fight for the possession of mares.
The mares mate during heat, and after eleven months of pregnancy, they give birth. Just half an hour later, the foal tries to stand up and begin the long march in search of new pastures.
As it grows, it may graze for up to 13 hours a day, sleep lying down—but only in safe places—and gallop to escape danger.

For most horses, life has changed greatly.
Horses are a highly adaptable species.
It is the human's duty to understand them in order to allow them a peaceful life in new environmental conditions—remembering that they are sensitive animals, easily influenced by human moods and by their surroundings.

CHAPTER 2: Morphological Types

By "type," we mean the structural characteristics that define the body of equines. The external conformation of the horse is an important evaluative element in determining the value of a quadruped and in classifying it according to a specific service, work, or sporting activity. 
The forms are only a first evaluative element; it is essential that the various regions forming the horse's external topography are proportioned according to the relationships defined by the morphological type under consideration.
The dolichomorphic type is characterized by light body mass, extended and rapid movements, and proportionate nervous excitability. This type has a natural aptitude for speed.
The brachymorphic type presents with abundant and voluminous muscle mass arranged perpendicularly to the bone levers, a broad body, and a wide support base. It is particularly suited for heavy and fast pulling.
The mesomorphic type, showing intermediate characteristics, combines strength and speed, and is therefore suitable for riding and light draft services.

2.1 The Head
The head should be proportionate and well set onto the neck; a head that is too large may become heavy and negatively affect the quality of movement, especially if it is poorly attached. The ears must be small and mobile. A well-shaped forehead should be flat and broad, and the eyes must be large, symmetrical, and wide open.
An eye is referred to as "cerchiato" when a white ring is visible around the shiny cornea. It is called "gazzuolo" when the light blue coloring of the front part of the iris is visible through the cornea, and "vaio" when it reflects a pearl gray color.
Since equines do not breathe through their mouths, the nostrils are the only airways available to the organism. Therefore, nostrils must be large and highly mobile.

2.2 The Teeth
In general, horses have 40 teeth in males and 36 in females, which usually lack canines or tushes. By observing the teeth, one can determine the horse's age. The toothless spaces between the first molars and the tushes on the lower jaw are called "bars," and they serve as the resting point for the bit.
The tongue and palate must be well proportioned and healthy to avoid discomfort when positioning the bit.

2.3 The Neck
The shape of the neck is particularly important. Aside from the base of support provided by the limbs, the neck acts as a balancer, which influences movement quality. The neck should generally be longer than the head, free to move in coordination with the trunk and head, and harmoniously connected to both while remaining clearly distinct.
The neck is described as straight or pyramidal when its margins extend in straight lines that converge from the trunk to the head. It is called arched when the upper margin is convex throughout its length, and swan-like when thin and arched. A neck is said to be ewe-like or deer-like when its upper margin is concave, a conformation that tends to impair movement and encourages the horse to go above the bit.
The "chopped neck" refers to a depression just in front of the withers along the upper neck margin. The "lance strike" is a small depression observed in some horses at the junction of the neck and shoulders. If pronounced, both features indicate poor neck attachment.

2.4 The Trunk
The thrust generated by the hindquarters is transmitted through the spine and sets the body mass in motion, making good trunk conformation essential. The muscles responsible for lifting the forelimb and moving the shoulders are attached at the withers. The back acts as a bridge between the front and rear parts of the body.
The back must be straight and broad. It should be short in horses with strong contraction capabilities or in pack animals, and somewhat longer—but not excessively so—in horses requiring extended contraction. A swaybacked conformation, with a pronounced curve, indicates poor load-bearing capacity and fragility.
The loin region must be broad, short, straight, and well attached to the croup. The croup itself should be long, well-muscled, and not completely horizontal in saddle horses. If too sloped, it is described as low or sunken. It is called double if the muscle masses on each side are divided by a longitudinal groove.
The hips should protrude slightly from the surrounding areas, be at equal height, and set apart from each other.

2.5 The Limbs
The limbs are the four pillars that support the trunk and allow locomotion. By convention, two limbs together form what is called a biped. Consequently, there are six bipeds: front, rear, two lateral, and two diagonal.
The limbs are composed of bone segments that are articulated with one another and moved by muscles attached to them. The forelimbs serve to elastically support the center of gravity and push it upward and forward. The hindlimbs assist in supporting the body and in propelling it forward. Being farther from the center of gravity, they bear less weight than the forelimbs but play a leading role in thrust.

2.6 Limb Alignment
To avoid excessive strain on joints, ligaments, and tendons from muscle-generated movement, the horse must have a proportionate body structure and a good support base on the ground. For this reason, limb alignment is crucial.
By "alignment," we mean the directional orientation of the limbs in relation to a vertical line. In general, alignment is considered good when the center of suspension and the center of support of the limb fall along the same vertical line, allowing the body to be supported and moved with optimal efficiency.

2.7 Skeletal Structure
The horse’s body consists of a rigid bone base, with the spine being the central structural element. The cervical section of the spine corresponds to the neck. The thoracic vertebrae, together with the ribs, form the rib cage, which supports the internal organs and connects the forelimbs and hindlimbs via the rest of the spine. The spine, therefore, plays a key role in movement.
The spine includes 18 thoracic vertebrae, 6 lumbar vertebrae, 5 sacral vertebrae fused together, and 18 coccygeal vertebrae. The 7 cervical vertebrae connect the head to the trunk and allow movement of the neck, which is essential for balance and propulsion. For example, during galloping, the neck moves in synchrony with the contraction and extension of the back.
The skeleton is a unified system made up of a series of bones interconnected by various joints. These joints are built differently depending on their function. In the lower limbs, below the elbow and knee, the main joints allow only flexion and extension along the longitudinal axis. Other joints, such as the hip, where the articular surfaces resemble concave and convex spherical segments and the capsule is thickened, allow greater mobility and lateral movements.
The pivot joint is another type, characterized by the rotation of one bone around its own longitudinal axis, as seen between the first two cervical vertebrae: the atlas and axis. In this case, a bony projection from the axis penetrates into the ring-shaped base of the atlas, where it is secured by ligaments. This allows the atlas to rotate and enables the lateral oscillation of the head.
The joint between the atlas and the skull allows vertical oscillation of the head through two condyles—hook-shaped protrusions. Intervertebral discs are found between each pair of vertebrae except for the first two cervical spaces. These are special cartilaginous joints that connect adjacent vertebral bodies.
The discs make up about a quarter of the spinal column's length. Each is made up of a gelatinous inner part surrounded by dense fibrous tissue that adheres firmly to the vertebrae. This unique structure allows a limited range of movement without compromising strength, acting as a shock absorber.
The movement between vertebrae includes dorsoventral flexion and extension, limited by the spinous processes; lateral flexion and extension, limited by the transverse processes in the lumbar area; and rotational movement, which is restricted by additional joints between the vertebral arches.
Ligaments prevent excessive or abnormal joint angles. Made of fibrous tissue, they are attached to the periosteum of the bones they connect. Muscles play a fundamental role in maintaining bone alignment: joint stability largely depends on the surrounding muscle groups. Unlike ligaments, muscles can remain under tension even during movement, whether they produce or resist it.
Muscle contraction, thanks to tendinous ends attached to bone, produces movement. This movement is limited by the ligament complex of the joint and by opposing muscle groups. In the horse, an important connection between different bones depends entirely on muscle: the connection between the forelimb and the trunk is made possible by muscles that run from the humerus to the scapula and from the neck to the thorax.

CHAPTER 3: Description and Identification of a Horse

The identification record is the description of external features used to recognize a horse and distinguish it from others.
These external characteristics are based on sex: a male horse is referred to as a "stallion" if it is approved for breeding; a female is referred to as a "mare" if used for reproduction; a "gelding" is a male horse from which the testicles have been removed.
Other elements considered include age, height, coat color, distinctive markings, breed, and origin.
The height of the horse must be declared, measured in centimetres from the ground to the withers. Height also differentiates horses from ponies: adult horses below 150 cm in height are classified as ponies.
Horses used for sport or recreational activities must have an identification document issued by equestrian organizations.

3.1 The Coat
The term "coat" refers to the combination of hair and mane covering the horse’s body, which varies in color and, along with distinctive markings, serves as a key for identification.

A black coat (morello) is characterized by a black base color, with black mane and tail.
A chestnut coat (sauro) is uniform in color, where the body, limbs, and mane are all the same reddish or golden hue.
A bay coat (baio) presents with two colors: the body is brown in varying shades, while the extremities and mane are black.
A grey coat is a mix of black and white hairs on black skin, evenly distributed. As the horse ages, the coat tends to lighten.
A blue roan coat (sorcino) features two colors: lead-coloured hairs, black mane, and a uniformly grey body.
A red roan coat (ubero) is a mix of reddish and white hairs.
A dun or mouse-grey coat (falbo or lupino) consists of a mixture of black and yellowish or reddish hairs, with black mane and extremities.
A roan coat is composed of black, red, and white mixed hairs, with mane and extremities usually shaded in black. The tone is referred to as light if white predominates, dark if black predominates, and wine-red if red predominates.
A piebald coat (pezzato) features large patches of color on a white background or vice versa. When the base color dominates, the name of the coat follows the word "piebald" (e.g., piebald chestnut, piebald bay); when white dominates, the name precedes it (e.g., chestnut piebald, bay piebald).
A tricolored coat (mantello coniugato) includes patches of three different colors.
An Isabella coat is made up of cream-yellow hairs, fading into black or darker tones at the extremities; the mane may be black.
A cream coat shares the same body color as Isabella, but the mane is the same color as the body.
A palomino coat has the same body color as Isabella, but the mane is white.
A white or albino horse is completely depigmented and has pink skin.

Additional descriptors are used to further specify the tone of the coat.
A bay may be called cherry bay when the color is bright and light, or dark bay when it is reddish-brown.
A grey may be described as "storno" when black predominates and white hairs appear as small scattered flecks, or as light grey when white hairs dominate over black ones.

The term rabicano refers to a bay, chestnut, or black coat that has sparse white hairs scattered over the entire body or parts of it.
The term zaino refers to a horse entirely lacking white hairs.
A dappled horse (pomellato) displays round spots that are lighter or darker than the rest of the coat.
A flecked coat (moscato) is scattered with tiny spots of black hairs.

Other traits that aid in identifying a horse include markings such as morfee—white or yellowish patches commonly found on areas with fine skin in darker coats. The dorsal stripe is a very dark stripe running along the back from withers to tail.
Accidental markings and branding are also considered.
The Appaloosa coat is a grey background especially on the hindquarters, dotted with black and brown spots.

3.2 Distinctive Markings
Distinctive markings are recorded in writing and on the diagram of the identification document in correspondence to their anatomical location.

Whorls (remolini) are changes in hair direction and may take various shapes such as simple, pointed, or wavy-pointed forms.
White markings may be regular or irregular and can be mixed into the coat hair either partially or along the edges.

A star is a white mark on the forehead, varying in size, shape, and position.
A stripe (lista) is a white hair line running down the face from the forehead to the nostrils.
White-mouthed (bevente in bianco) refers to a horse with white lips, which may occur on both sides, on the upper or lower lip, and can be full or partial.
Smooth (liscio) refers to a pink patch covered in fine hair, generally located on the nose or lips, and it may or may not be connected to the stripe.
A sock (balzana) is a white marking on the limbs extending upward from the coronet to varying heights. Depending on how far it extends, it takes on different specific names.

CHAPTER 4: Hygiene and Prevention

4.1 The Stable
Often, the horse is forced to spend long hours of its day confined in a stable; the box in which it lives is therefore a very important place and must have characteristics of safety and comfort.
First of all, space: the larger, the better. In any case, it should not be smaller than 3 meters by 3 meters, preferably 3.50 meters by 3.50 meters.
Some horses, especially younger ones unfamiliar with this unnatural home, may assume positions when lying down that make it difficult for them to get back up due to the box walls. A larger area or the addition of a raised bedding along the sides or a wall where they can push against helps prevent this dangerous issue.
The "bowls"—automatic water dispensers—should be placed at a suitable height, easily accessible to the horse, but not too low, and preferably near a corner so they are not in the way should the horse roll while lying down.
The manger should also be placed in a corner, constructed without sharp edges and low enough for the horse to eat in a natural position, without food contamination.
Bowls and mangers must be kept clean.
It is important that the box is not a dusty place because inhaling dust over long periods can cause respiratory issues or trigger allergic reactions. The stable must be well-ventilated with high ceilings, while avoiding drafts that could hit the horse directly and risk bronchitis.
The true enemy of the athletic horse is boredom.
For this reason, stables built so that horses can see each other and look outside are the best.
In any case, if doors do not have an upper grate, they should be reasonably high so that the horse cannot lean on them with its chest, which can cause abrasions.
There are numerous ways to distract a bored horse or one affected by vices such as “weaving” or “cribbing,” but the best remedy remains movement.
The materials used for bedding are quite varied, ranging from straw to wood shavings, rice husks, and even processed newspaper.
Each material has its advantages and disadvantages and requires different care.
In all cases, the bedding must be soft, dry, as clean as possible, and must not force the standing horse into unnatural positions, which can happen if the bedding is uneven.
Periodically, it must be entirely removed, and the floor as well as the walls must be disinfected, as should the corridors be disinfected frequently.
It should be remembered that the majority of hoof diseases derive from poor stable hygiene.
Horses can also be stabled in tie stalls measuring 3 meters in length and no less than 1.70 meters in width.
This type of stabling considerably reduces the horse’s ability to move and imposes some safety standards to allow the horse to lie down.
It requires the horse to always wear a halter attached to a rope that passes through a ring, at the other end of which is a counterweight that acts as a stopper being larger than the ring.
The practicality of this type of housing comes entirely at the expense of the horse’s comfort and well-being.

4.2 Feeding
The horse has adapted to a very different dietary regime than its natural one.
The first thing to consider is the relationship between fuel food—energy value, the quantity of feed—and consumption, that is, the athletic effort the horse is subjected to.
Equally important is the obvious relationship between the quantity of food and the weight of the horse.
The choice of feed and how to distribute it throughout the day must consider how the horse digests.
Salivary glands attached to the mouth begin the digestive process through enzymes.
From the pharynx begins a cylindrical canal called the esophagus, which leads to the stomach.
The stomach holds about 15 litres on average, small compared to the size of the animal; it must fill and empty before the ration is fully consumed.
The stomach connects the esophagus to the small intestine, which is linked to two large glands: the liver and the pancreas. The small intestine leads to the large intestine, subdivided into cecum, colon, and rectum.
The cecum is very large and important because it serves as a reservoir for liquid feed, which the horse greatly needs.
The daily food quantity required must be divided into as many portions as possible, compatible with the stable’s management, bearing in mind that the initial digestive process lasts at least 90 minutes during which the horse should not be subjected to exertion that would reduce the blood flow needed for digestion.
The feeds commonly given to horses are many.
To provide only some general and therefore generic guidelines, one can say that a horse weighing about 500 kg in moderate work can be maintained with about 5 kg of cereals (barley and oats, for example) and 8 kg of hay per day.
Barley and oats can be fed as whole grains, crushed, or flaked.
Whole grains retain all nutrients, unlike crushed or flaked, but some unmasticated grains can be found undigested in the feces.
Wheat and corn, although high in nutrients, are not recommended as staple feeds for horses because they are poorly digestible but can be used as energy supplements based on the horse’s workload.
Vegetable oils are also used to increase fat content in the horse’s diet.
Hay must be low in dust, free of mold, and sourced from uncontaminated fields.
There are many types of grasses and legumes used for hay, each with different properties.
Generally, first-cut hay is preferred; that which is usually harvested and dried in May, known as “maggengo,” is among the richest in minerals and fiber.
Bran, when mixed with crushed grains cooked in ample water, in moderate amounts, is beneficial because it facilitates bowel emptying.
A periodic mash aids digestive processes, but the composition and frequency of mash feeding must be regulated by experienced personnel to avoid intestinal issues.
Supplementing dry feed with carrots and beets is pleasant for the horse and encourages appetite.
Transitioning from a dry to a fresh green diet (fresh pasture) must be done gradually to prevent digestive problems.
Water is by far the most important nutrient; it must not be too cold or contaminated. The most practical solution is an automatic watering system.
If watering manually, it should be offered frequently throughout the day, before feeding to avoid interfering with digestion, providing 20–30 litres of water depending on the climate.
In any case, avoid letting the horse drink cold water after work while it is still hot.
When feeding the horse, never forget to provide salt; the horse will regulate its intake according to its needs.

4.3 Grooming
The horse’s body must be cleaned regularly.
In nature, horses roll, scratch, and groom each other.
The massage effect of the curry comb and stiff brush not only meets hygiene needs but is also pleasant and stimulating for the horse’s muscles.
It is also a good way to build trust, as the horse gets used to the human’s scent and touch.
The limbs, muzzle, and sensitive areas must be brushed gently; neck, back, croup, and ribs can be massaged with the curry comb and brush.
The hooves must be cleaned before and after work to remove stones and debris from the frog and sole, and to remove manure and urine that, when packed into the frog, can cause thrush.
To retain moisture in the hoof wall and maintain hoof elasticity, the hoof must be greased periodically.
The tail should be washed often and brushed gently and infrequently to avoid hair loss. The mane should be thinned so as not to interfere with the reins and to facilitate braiding.
A cloth is used for the final cleaning, and a sponge for delicate areas.
In winter, it is advisable to protect horses with blankets to shield them from cold and dampness; blanketing is necessary for clipped horses.
Partial or complete clipping of the long winter coat is done in autumn (October or November, depending on the climate) to prevent horses in work from sweating excessively and then cooling down too slowly.
Showers and baths in summer support thermoregulation and help maintain coat cleanliness. Drying horses is not always easy; it is best to scrape off water with a sweat scraper and then walk the horse.
When trimming excess hair, one must consider the protective function that hair and mane have over the areas they cover.
The forelock should not be overly shortened, the hairs protruding from the ear should only be trimmed slightly, and the tail hair can be cut to the level of the hock.

4.4 Approaching Horses
The horse is not aggressive towards humans; if it appears so, it is likely due to abuse or a fearful nature.
Respecting safety rules and building mutual trust are key to approaching a horse calmly.
Giving the horse time to understand who it is dealing with is a good rule: let it smell the person, see who is approaching, and ensure there are no sudden, high movements or hesitant actions that could be misinterpreted. The horse should not experience unnecessary pain or restraints.

4.5 Dental Prevention
Regular dental checks are important, as uneven wear on molars can cause painful sharp edges which, over time, create serious problems with chewing and digestion.
These edges may injure the inside of the cheeks and cause discomfort with the bit.
A six-month dental check-up by a vet will allow, if necessary, to smooth out sharp edges and remove tartar, keeping the entire digestive system efficient.

4.6 Deworming
Deworming is necessary to prevent and treat infestations of small parasitic worms found in plant-based food, especially in pastures, where their population is constantly increased by manure containing eggs and larvae.

4.7 Tetanus Vaccine
The tetanus bacillus is found in horse feces, and its spores are extremely resistant, reproducing only in anaerobic conditions.
It becomes very dangerous when it enters a wound and finds the conditions to multiply.
It produces a powerful toxin that spreads through the nervous system and can lead to death.
The vaccine must be administered annually, with the first dose followed by a booster after one month.
In case of a serious wound, immediately administer the serum and ensure the wound is well disinfected and oxygenated.

4.8 Influenza Vaccine
To combat the most dangerous strains of influenza viruses, there are vaccines that must be administered on a regular annual basis.

4.9 Shoeing
Shoeing is not necessary for wild horses but is very important for sport horses. It protects the hoof when working on varied terrain, improves traction through studs, and allows correction of conformation and gait issues via trimming (hoof cutting).
The choice of shoeing method, and more importantly trimming, is crucial for proper athletic performance.
Shoeing can be done cold, where the farrier shapes the shoe after trimming. In hot shoeing, the shoe is tried on while hot, helping it fit perfectly to the hoof.
This method results in a more secure fit and better distribution of pressure, but care must be taken not to burn or inflame the sensitive parts of the hoof.
Shoeing can correct defects in hoof proportion, shape, and wall quality.
The farrier will adjust the trim and shape of the shoe to encourage healthy hoof growth.
A hoof that is too small and contracted requires wide-webbed shoes, heels should not be trimmed, and the frog must be preserved.
A flat hoof needs the sole preserved and a covered, bevelled shoe.
A soft hoof demands careful trimming, while a dry hoof is better with a shoe with few nail holes to avoid cracks.
In some hoof pathologies causing deformation, proper shoeing can relieve pain.
All shoeing that alters the hoof’s shape is called corrective shoeing.
Shoes are usually replaced every forty days, but growth rate and trimming needs vary per horse.

4.10 Pathologies
The main locomotor disorders show up as lameness or localized swelling, often at the site of injury; rest and cold water applications are advisable while waiting for the vet.
Creams or stable wraps should only be used with veterinary approval.
Lameness usually signals pain, but there can also be mechanical lameness due to joint stiffness or muscle contractions without pain.
Bleeding wounds require immediate veterinary intervention.
Meanwhile, the wound can be rinsed with cold water, disinfected, and bandaged.
If bleeding is rhythmic with heartbeats, it is arterial and very serious—apply a tourniquet upstream if possible.
Digestive system disorders also need quick veterinary care.
Colic causes intense pain shown by pawing the ground, looking at the flank, lying down, or pressing the abdomen.
The horse must be fasted, kept calm (especially if lying down), kept warm, and possibly given antispasmodics or painkillers.
Respiratory dysfunction may show as coughing, nasal discharge, or laboured breathing; avoid dust and temperature swings and await the vet.
Daily skin checks allow early intervention in skin diseases and allergic reactions.
At the first sign of hives, blisters, or abnormal growths, consult the vet. Fungal infections are contagious.
Careful hoof inspection helps detect thrush, cracks, and white line disease early.

CHAPTER 5: Movement

Movement is essential to life and is necessary to carry out all those operations (searching for food, escaping dangers, etc.) that ultimately serve to preserve and reproduce the species.
In sport, movement is aimed at achieving an athletic result.
The best way to achieve an athletic result is to use natural movements, enhance them, and adapt them to athletic requirements.
What creates movement is the nerve impulse that, from the brain through a complex system of nerves, stimulates fibrous tissues that react differently depending on the nature of the stimulus, their structure, and the condition of adequate energy supply guaranteed by the cardiovascular system.
These fibrous tissues are the muscles, the agents of movement.
The muscle works by contracting isometrically when it does not change its length, concentrically when it shortens, and eccentrically when it lengthens.
Isometric contractions occur when the horse must maintain a position or when resisting traction with a fixed joint.
Concentric contractions occur when a lever is activated to change a position.
Eccentric contractions occur when the contractions oppose a counteracting force.
A typical example is the action of the forelimb muscles when a horse lands after a jump: the muscle tends to lengthen as it controls the descent of the fetlock.

Movement arises from lever systems.
There are levers where the fulcrum is between force and load, and others where the force is applied between the fulcrum and the load.
In the first case (elbow or hock), powerful muscle masses are required to produce broad and fast movements.
In the second case (forearm, tibia), effort and speed are lower.

5.1 Energy systems

The energy supply needed for the functioning of systems, maintaining body structure, and movement comes from food broken down and synthesized into sugars (from starches) and proteins.
The chemical process that transforms food into usable energy (ATP: adenosine triphosphate) can occur in the presence or absence of oxygen.
In the latter case, ATP production also generates waste substances that must be eliminated.
There are therefore different systems for energy production.
The anaerobic alactic system allows for limited efforts over short periods.
If the effort continues over time, the aerobic system (with oxygen) comes into play, which can last a very long time as long as the intensity of the effort allows the respiratory and cardiovascular systems to supply sufficient oxygen for ATP production.
This system produces clean waste (water and carbon dioxide).
If the energy demand is very high and the aerobic system can no longer meet the requirement, the anaerobic lactic system is activated, generating a significant amount of energy but for a short time.
Food is transformed into energy through elaborate chemical processes.
This transformation produces heat, as evidenced by the fact that the rectal temperature of a healthy, resting adult horse averages 37°–38°, but if the horse is ill or working intensely, its temperature rises.

5.2 Respiration

An important fuel for the animal engine is oxygen, found in the air and essential for producing energy via the aerobic system.
Respiration occurs through the nasal cavities, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and lungs.
By inhaling, the horse takes in oxygen, and by exhaling, expels waste.
An adult horse at rest performs 9 to 13 respiratory acts per minute, but under effort, these increase significantly to meet greater fuel demands.
The abdominal organs are suspended in the abdominal vault by their own ligaments, and during galloping, they oscillate due to inertia and the movement itself.
They move forward with the anterior lowering of the longitudinal axis, favouring exhalation.
With the extension of the hindquarters pushing off, the inhalation phase begins.
The organs thus act as a piston that reduces the workload of the diaphragm, which normally has to move a mass weighing over 100 kg to decompress the lungs.
As a result, during galloping, breathing is synchronized with the gait and allows for energy savings in a high-consumption situation.
This energy saving ceases whenever the gallop rhythm changes, because the visceral piston, which moves by inertia, needs time to adapt its rhythm.
It also ceases based on the gradient of the terrain due to the force of gravity preventing inertial motion.

5.3 Cardiovascular system

The component responsible for delivering fuel to all tissues and parts of the body is the blood, which is pumped by the heart.
Heart rate increases based on fuel needs, so a resting horse has about 35–40 beats per minute, but in activity or illness, the number of beats increases.
If a resting horse pumps just under a liter per contraction, under effort the output can reach around 40 liters.
The demand for greater blood transport is met by increasing the heart rate, which can reach 200–240 beats per minute, and by increasing the amount of blood pumped per contraction, which can rise from 15 to 40 liters.
Beyond 220–240 beats, the heart no longer has time to fill sufficiently and expel the blood it contains, resulting in a significant drop in efficiency and dangerous conditions.

5.4 Training and schooling

The purpose of training is to prepare the musculature to act quickly and effectively, dosing effort while consuming as little as possible.
Specific training improves the movement itself, while also enhancing the ability of the body systems to cope with the strain involved.
Training must be progressive and require a step-by-step increase in workload.
The more varied the movement experiences in youth, the easier it will be to plan a schooling and training program for the horse.
The more a muscle is exercised, the more its fibers increase in strength and size.
Conversely, if underused, it will progressively atrophy.
Analysing movement mechanics, we observe that propulsion from the hind limbs is transmitted to the spine, whose availability is essential to effectively receive this thrust that enables the body mass to advance.
Movements of the spine in the vertical plane consist of straightening and arching into a ventrally concave arc.
A spine that tends to be dorsally concave (lordosis) loses much of its functional capacity.
The abdominal muscles are the string, and the spine is the bow that stores energy.
Therefore, a horse engaged with the hindquarters under the body is like a bow ready to release force.

We can imagine that a horse has two muscle chains.
One runs along the top from the neck along the back to the thighs.
The other runs below from the abdomen to the sternum.
When the upper chain contracts, the horse pushes with the hind legs, extending them back to open the joints and apply energy, and the spine straightens while the neck stretches.
When the lower chain contracts, the horse stores energy.
The spine arches, the hindquarters are drawn under the mass, and the neck rises.
The upper chain is much stronger than the lower chain.
To allow the horse to store energy, the upper chain must be relaxed.

For these reasons, the horse's musculature must be exercised comprehensively while always considering elasticity and the ability to relax, especially in the upper areas such as the longissimus dorsi.
Excessive strengthening of muscle groups may lead to stiffness.
Insufficient training would wear down the structure, especially when engaging in activities like jumping or collected gaits, and in any case, it would reduce the horse’s ability to carry the rider’s weight.
The neck plays a major role in movement.
Its vertical flexion stimulates and allows the flexion of the thoracolumbar segment.
Its horizontal flexion balances the horizontal flexion of the thoracolumbar segment in gaits where this is required (e.g., walk).

When the horse lowers its head, it stretches the nuchal ligament.
This causes traction on the spinous processes, opening up the vertebrae, and the spine rises, increasing its arch and load-bearing capacity.
The head and neck significantly affect the position of the center of gravity.
The center of gravity shifts forward when the head is positioned low and forward, and shifts backward when the head and neck are raised.
The more the hindquarters are engaged under the body, the further back the center of gravity moves.
The center of gravity of a standing horse is located in the front part of the thoracic region, so most of the body weight rests on the front limbs.
Movement is a continuous loss and recovery of balance.
This happens because, as the mass moves forward, it reaches new support bases created by the coordinated movement of limb placement and lift-off.

The goal of schooling is to improve the horse’s ability to manage balance.
Movement in a balanced state requires less effort.
Another goal is to promote relaxation and muscular elasticity because stiffness increases exertion and reduces agility.

CHAPTER 6: Gait Patterns

The horse’s movement is expressed in the three gaits of walk, trot, and canter.
In these three gaits, the horse arranges its limbs differently, thereby advancing its mass as defined by the amount of ground covered through the coordinated movements of its limbs and body, and the frequency with which these occur.
Each gait has a motor pattern alternating lift-offs, landings, and ground contacts of the four limbs, creating support polygons made up of one or more limbs.
As the horse's mass advances and modifies its support polygons, the position of its center of gravity shifts.
In order to maintain balance, the mass itself—especially the neck, acting as a counterbalance—must contribute to bringing the center of gravity back over the base of support.
In the so-called swinging gaits (walk and canter), the neck balances by stretching with the hind limb's lift; in the walk, it horizontally balances the slight flexion of the ribcage that occurs when the horse bears its weight on a single lateral biped.

6.1 The Walk
The walk is described in the international dressage rules, article 403.
The walk is a "four-beat" gait in which the horse’s limbs land one after the other, in clearly marked and consistent rhythm maintained throughout all work at the walk.
When the front and hind legs on the same side land almost simultaneously, the walk tends toward a lateral movement.
This irregularity is a serious fault in the gait.

There are four types of walk: collected walk, medium walk, extended walk, and free walk.

In the collected walk, the horse, maintained “on the bit”, moves forward resolutely with a raised and rounded neck, clearly showing it can sustain balance on its own.
The head position approaches the vertical; the contact with the mouth remains soft.
The hind limbs engage well, with good movement of the hocks.
The gait remains a “walk” and is energetic, with a regular sequence of footfalls.
Each step covers less ground than in the medium walk but is higher due to greater flexion of each joint, showing self-carriage.
It is shorter than the medium walk but more active.

The medium walk is a clear, regular, and relaxed walk of moderate extension.
The horse, kept “on the bit”, walks energetically yet calmly, with even and purposeful steps, the hind feet stepping in front of the prints left by the forefeet.
The rider maintains a light, soft, and steady contact with the horse’s mouth.

In the extended walk, the horse covers as much ground as possible without rushing or altering the regularity of the beat.
The hind hooves land well in front of the tracks left by the front hooves.
The rider allows the horse to stretch the neck and move the head forward, without losing contact with the mouth.

The free walk is a relaxed gait where the horse is given full freedom to lower the head and stretch the neck.

6.2 The Trot
The trot is described in the international dressage rules, article 404.
The trot is a "two-beat" gait separated by a moment of suspension, in which the horse moves in diagonal pairs, with simultaneous support from the front and hind limb of the same diagonal (e.g., left front with right hind, and vice versa).
The trot must always be free-moving, active, and regular in rhythm and should be initiated without hesitation.

Trot quality is judged by overall impression, regularity, and elasticity of the strides—these come from a supple back and good hindquarter engagement, as well as the ability to maintain the same rhythm and natural balance even after a transition from one type of trot to another.

There are four types of trot: collected trot, working trot, medium trot, and extended trot.

In the collected trot, the horse, kept “on the bit”, moves forward with a raised and rounded neck.
The hocks, well engaged, preserve impulsion energy, allowing the shoulders to move freely in all directions.
The strides are shorter than in other trots, but the horse is lighter and more mobile.

The working trot is an intermediate gait between the collected and medium trot.
A horse not yet trained or ready for collection shows good balance in this gait.
Maintained “on the bit”, the horse moves forward with regular, elastic strides and active hips.
The expression "active hips" doesn’t mean collection is required—it emphasizes the importance of impulsion from the hindquarters.

The medium trot is an intermediate gait between the working trot and the extended trot but is “rounder” than the extended.
The horse moves forward freely, moderately lengthening its strides with clear impulsion from behind.
The rider allows the horse, kept “on the bit”, to bring the head slightly in front of the vertical compared to the collected or working trot, and to lower the head and neck slightly.
Strides should be as regular as possible, and the movement as a whole balanced and fluent.

In the extended trot, the horse covers the greatest possible distance.
Maintaining the same cadence, it maximally lengthens the stride due to strong hindquarter impulsion.
The rider allows the horse, kept “on the bit”, to lower and extend the neck without seeking support on the bit to avoid a high-headed movement.
The front hooves should not land behind their projected path on the ground.
The fore- and hindlimbs must extend similarly (more or less parallel) during the forward reach.

6.3 The Canter
The canter is described in the international dressage rules, article 405.
The canter is a "three-beat" gait in which, for example at the right canter, the beats occur in the following sequence: left hind, left diagonal (left fore and right hind simultaneously), right fore, followed by a suspension phase before the next stride.
The canter must always have regular, rhythmic strides performed with lightness and be initiated without hesitation.

Canter quality is judged by overall impression, regularity, and the lightness of the “three beats”, deriving from acceptance of the bit, a supple poll, and active engagement from behind via the hips, as well as the ability to maintain rhythm and natural balance even after transitioning between canter types.
The horse must always remain completely straight on straight lines.

There are four types of canter: collected, working, medium, and extended.

In the collected canter, the horse, kept “on the bit”, moves with a raised and rounded neck.
This gait is characterized by lightness in the forehand and engagement from behind: free, supple shoulders and very active hips.
The strides are shorter than in other canters, but the horse is lighter and more mobile.

The working canter is an intermediate gait between the collected and medium canter.
In this gait, a horse not yet trained or ready for collection shows good balance.
Kept “on the bit”, it moves forward with regular, light, and rhythmic strides, with active hips.
The phrase “active hips” does not imply obligatory collection but highlights the importance of impulsion from behind.

The medium canter is an intermediate gait between the working and extended canter.
The horse moves forward with determination, maintaining balance, and moderately lengthens its strides with clear impulsion from behind.
The rider allows the horse, kept “on the bit”, to place its head slightly in front of the vertical compared to collected or working canter, and also to slightly lower the head and neck.
Strides must be extended and as regular as possible.
The movement overall must be balanced and fluid.

In the extended canter, the horse covers the greatest distance possible.
Maintaining rhythm, it maximally lengthens its strides without losing calmness or lightness, thanks to strong impulsion from behind.
The rider allows the horse, kept “on the bit”, to lower and extend the head and neck.
The nose may extend forward, without seeking support on the bit.

6.4 Stride Length
This term refers to the portion of ground covered by each stride, that is, the horizontal distance on the ground between two consecutive prints of the same hoof.
A shorter stride length does not always correspond to a faster rhythm; if the horse knows how to carry weight, even while covering less ground, it will lift its body mass more clearly off the ground while maintaining the same rhythm.
This ability, especially in the jumping gaits, indicates the presence of impulsion.

6.5 Impulsion
Impulsion is the result of the force generated by muscle contraction followed by extension.
Thanks to the angular structure of certain joint segments in the horse’s hindquarters (e.g., thigh - leg - cannon), these can be considered as real rigid springs.
The force produced by the thrust of the hind limbs is directed obliquely from the bottom upwards and breaks down into two forces: one is almost completely dispersed through the upper limbs, while the other tends to run parallel to the body's axis.
This latter force is what we properly call impulsion.
This force finds in the spine the rigid axis that allows the mass to move forward.
Due to gravity, the force tends to be transmitted to the ground through the forelimbs, although a component of this forward projection remains in the head and neck, forming the horse’s contact on the bit.
In order for the horse to use the thrust from the hindquarters effectively, it must be muscularly symmetrical, i.e., “straight” both on straight lines and on curves.
A horse is straight when the front and hind legs travel on the same line in the chosen direction.
This element is a prerequisite for balance and regularity and is essential for impulsion.
A crooked horse directs the force from the hind limbs obliquely in relation to the body mass.
This results in energy dispersion and difficulty maintaining the intended direction of movement.
More effort, fewer results.
At the base of this complex mechanism must be a psychological willingness to sustain the effort.
A desire to move forward which, although part of the definition of impulsion, is also to some extent a component of submission.
It is a willing attitude toward athletic effort that does not stem from the need to satisfy a basic necessity—drinking, eating—nor from the threat of punishment.
But from a psychological condition of trust in the human and a willingness to accept the role assigned by them.
In summary, impulsion refers to the full willingness to work, demonstrated by engagement of the hindquarters and the elasticity of the back, expressed qualitatively in the ability to carry and push weight without loss of balance and in proportion to the level of training.
The ability to push weight and to carry weight are equally important.
Both must be present in a horse with good impulsion.
It is not possible to request an improvement in the ability to carry weight, as in collected gaits, without the ability to push weight, as in medium and extended gaits.

6.6 Submission
Submission and impulsion cannot be separated.
They are two closely connected aspects of the horse’s athletic expression.
A lack of calm willingness to work prevents any possible improvement in athletic performance.
A balanced and symmetrical muscular development, the optimization of energy consumption.
Only good submission allows for the relaxation necessary to produce quality impulsion.
The willing obedience of a horse with a good degree of submission is expressed through its acceptance of the rider’s leg and even more precisely through the relationship it develops with the rider’s hand.
On the contact taken by the rider with the horse’s mouth through the reins, the horse gives a light and steady support.
The horse that resists the hand, rushing the rhythm of its gaits and overloading the forehand, wastes energy without any benefit to movement.
The horse against the hand does not allow lightness in the forehand.
It thus becomes less responsive to transition requests and changes of direction.
The horse against the hand stiffens the masseter, which in turn causes stiffness in the neck and back muscles.
A horse that goes above the bit hollows its back and loses much of the thrust from the hindquarters.
It experiences an unpleasant sensation that can lead to actual pathologies.
A horse that evades the bit loses a large portion of the horizontal force generated by the hindquarters.
Even in the case of a horse behind the bit, stiffness in the jaw and a lowered back may result.
An excellent contact from the rider and a strong forward-driving leg aid are essential to counter a horse that “incenses”—i.e., that moves its head without providing steady contact on the bit.
The horse that swings sideways pushes with one hind leg outside the body’s centreline.
The haunches that drift outward prevent the hind leg from pushing under the body, overload the inside shoulder, and ultimately interfere with correct circle work.
Such evasions are often linked to physical or mental injuries and require excellent skills to resolve.
The horse’s willing cooperation arises from positive experiences.
When work results in muscle tone and greater resistance to effort, it brings a physical pleasure that is easily perceived by the horse as well as by us.
Just as inadequate effort or harsh handling produces pain that discourages repeating the movement.

6.7 Cadence
Cadence is described in the international dressage rules, article 401/7.
Cadence is the expression of the particular harmony a horse demonstrates when moving with clearly marked regularity, impulsion, and balance.
The rhythm the horse maintains in all its gaits is an integral part of cadence.
Cadence must be maintained in all exercises and in all variations within each gait.

6.8 Speed
Speed is the measurement of an object’s movement through space over a given period of time.
The speed of a horse’s gaits is the product of stride frequency and stride length.
To increase speed within the same gait, the horse increases both components simultaneously, but each evolves in its own way.
Functionally, the control of frequency and amplitude is governed by distinct nervous mechanisms.
Frequency is triggered by stimulation from the central nervous system.
Amplitude results directly from the number of muscle fibers available in each active muscle.
If there is insufficient muscular availability to increase stride length, a request for more speed will lead either to a shift to a faster-patterned gait or, if this is not possible, to an increase in frequency—depending on various energy cost considerations.
Each gait has an optimal speed at which energy consumption is minimal.
This varies greatly depending on the horse’s athletic conditioning and conformation.
Left free to choose, the horse will adopt the most economical solution, adapting its movement strategy to the consistency and profile of the terrain.
In general, repeating a locomotor movement requires a greater energy investment than increasing its amplitude.

CHAPTER 7: Jumping

7.1 The Phases of the Jump

One of the horse’s most fascinating athletic gestures is the jump.
Whether the horse jumps in nature by free choice is debatable, but that it can do so with ease is certain and proven.
Undoubtedly, it is an effort that can cause wear depending on the morphology more or less suitable for this type of exercise, how it is ridden, the type of obstacle, and the characteristics of the ground.
For all these reasons, training that enables the horse to execute a jump easily and naturally is of great importance.

The phases of greatest stress are: THE CALL-UP PHASE OF THE HINDQUARTERS OR TAKE-OFF, THE PROPULSION PHASE, THE AERIAL OR PARABOLIC PHASE, THE LANDING PHASE.

The call-up phase is the one that immediately follows the forelimb stride and precedes the take-off from the ground in front of the obstacle.
During the forelimb stride, the horse gathers the hindquarters under itself to load maximum power.
Thus, there is an alteration in the canter rhythm.
While the succession of forelimb strides remains according to the gait pattern, the diagonal that determines the second beat of the canter disengages.

At the same time, there is a flexion of the thoraco-lumbar region that prepares the hindquarters for the jump.
The work of the neck is important because, by lowering, it facilitates the stretching of the thoraco-lumbar tract and thus allows the hindquarters to gain space, while by raising, in coordination with the thrust of the forelimbs, it performs a rebalancing action and allows the elevation of the forehand that prepares the second phase.

In the propulsion phase, the muscular masses of the trunk tense up, ready to spring.
After the propulsion of the hindquarters and the consequent take-off, follows the necessary extension of the thoraco-lumbar region, whose elasticity is essential for a correct and decisive action of the hindquarters.
The extension of the neck allows adequate extension of the thoraco-lumbar region and ensures the collection of the forelimbs during take-off.

In the parabola, two moments can be distinguished.
In the initial part, the ascending phase, the anterior section of the spine is greatly engaged.
At the peak of the parabola, all the vertebrae are solicited, in particular the cervico-thoracic and lumbosacral joints.
In the ascending phase, the work of the neck allows the flexed forelimbs to pass easily.

An unsuitable conformation (large head, short neck), or the presence of osteoarticular or ligamentous pain, makes this phase difficult.
It is very important that the forelimbs are as flexed as possible to determine a less cumbersome trajectory.
At this moment, the joints of the carpus, elbow, and shoulder are at work: pain in these areas or imperfect joint conformation makes the task difficult.
The horizontal disposition of the shoulder is what allows maximum flexion of the elbow, carpus, and fetlock.
A structurally straight shoulder will have less ability to position itself horizontally.

In some cases, horses can be observed moving part of the limb laterally or inward; in this case, flexion is not impaired and thus the capacity for collection is not compromised.

In the parabola, the reciprocal movement of the neck, thorax, and loins is essential to avoid muscle and vertebral strains.
Risky situations occur more frequently when the horse is tired and fatigued.
Fatigue compromises proper neuromuscular synchronization, and wrong movements are easily made.
In this situation, fatigued muscles have lost the elasticity necessary to control and correct possible errors.

The two joints, cervico-thoracic and lumbosacral, allow, within the limits of vertebral ligaments, movements not only on the longitudinal plane but also on the horizontal plane.
This allows both hindquarters to shift laterally, gaining space above the obstacle.
Every movement of lateralization and rotation of the trunk during the parabola affects the extension-flexion play of the spine and is achieved through a complex intervention of the back and abdominal muscle masses.

As one can easily imagine, it is a movement that requires considerable effort and, over time, significant wear of the spine.

The parabola concludes with the descending phase.
The transition from the culminating phase to the descending phase is extremely delicate.
The extension of the forelimbs, simultaneous with the descending phase of the spine, is extremely complex because almost all of the horse’s joint systems are involved.
The descent of the scapula accompanies the extension of the forelimbs, which thus prepare to absorb the impact with the ground.

The hindquarters, still engaged above the obstacle, are involved in flexion through the hip, stifle, and hock joints.
In the action of the hindquarters at this stage, three natural behaviors can be recognized that reveal different degrees of aptitude:

In the first case, the hindleg is fully flexed, carried forward under itself and, in order not to hit the obstacle, the horse must overextend the trunk.
This is the worst aptitude because of the exaggerated strain it imposes on the horse.

In the second case, the hindleg is maximally flexed under the pelvis, requiring a high and well-centered parabola.

In the third case, the hindleg shows good flexion of the stifle and hock, but the opening of the hip allows it to move slightly backward, thus gaining space without requiring unnecessary compensatory effort from the spine.

The fourth and final phase of the jump is landing, whose correct execution is essential for the horse to regain balance and maintain trajectory and speed.
The critical moment regarding balance is precisely that determined by impact with the ground.
The center of gravity, projected forward in the aerial phase, must here be repositioned backward relative to the mass, which remains in forward motion.

The activity of the spine is closely related to the position assumed by the forelimbs.
There is a first forelimb contact immediately followed by the other (third beat of the canter).
The first forelimb to touch the ground is not the one that determines the lead of the canter.
This is followed by a suspension phase in which spinal flexion allows the hindquarters to gather and then push off with one of them, according to the canter sequence, which will then be immediately followed by the diagonal stride, and so on.

In this phase, the neck, and therefore the weight of the head, lowers, reducing stress on the thoraco-lumbar tract.
At the same time, it facilitates shock absorption by the limbs through the following mechanism:
At the moment of impact, there is a relative descent of the trunk in relation to the limbs—a true shock absorber created by the action of the shoulder and scapula muscles (supraspinatus and infraspinatus).
Another shock absorber is the pastern.

During ground contact, there is a locking of the joint angles of the limb at every level, from the shoulder to the fetlock.
There is a passive elasticity provided by ligaments and tendons, and an active elasticity provided by the antagonistic contraction of flexor and extensor muscles.

During the subsequent suspension phase and spinal flexion, the hindquarters are fully extended.
This is an extremely delicate phase for the thigh muscles and all the fasciae that act between the spine and the hindquarters.
The neck is ready to raise again according to the well-known canter scheme.
The subsequent propulsion puts stress on the hock and the stifle joint.

7.2 Take-off Area

At the peak of the parabola, beyond the attitudinal and morphological differences of each horse, the position taken depends greatly on the type of jump.
For example, in jumping a water jump, the horse does not need to gain much height.
In executing a high jump, the horse lowers its neck further to ensure maximum flexion of the various sections of the spine.
In a long jump, the hindquarters engaged in the take-off phase will land behind the forelimb tracks.
In a very vertical jump, the hindquarters will reach or surpass the forelimb tracks.

Depending on the type of jump (vertical or wide), there is an optimal distance for the forelimbs to take off from the ground, which can schematically be indicated as slightly more than the obstacle’s height for a vertical jump, and the height plus half the width for a wide jump.

This distance (take-off area) is very variable when the jump is small.
The ability to interpret the take-off area decreases as the height of the jump increases.
Naturally, height, construction of the obstacle, and placement on the ground contribute to making this optimal distance variable.

In jumps with elevation changes, for example, the ascending phase is much longer than the descending one.
In uphill jumps, the exact opposite occurs.

CHAPTER 8: Confidence

The relationship between man and horse can only arise if the mass ratio between the two allows the former to take care of the latter, fostering the necessary confidence.
Therefore, it is appropriate that a child, for example, should interact with a pony of gentle temperament, which he can groom, touch, feed, and spend time with — always under the watchful eye of the instructor, who can stimulate his curiosity towards the horse by teaching him the practice of basic handling.

When riding a horse, situations of tension occur, such as anxiety caused by the horse’s possible reactions, the lack of balance and orientation, and the search for effective action.
The horse’s height and the resulting distance from the ground create these states of insecurity.

There are various methods to introduce beginners to equestrian practice.
Each method is successful if applied in a way that the rider can understand and if it uses appropriate means.

In the first approach, situations of speed and control (directional control, anxiety linked to the search for effective action) must be avoided.
The use of docile horses in a section, with a reliable leader, avoids at least part of these risks.

Work with a horse on the lunge, equipped with a handle and without a saddle, as in vaulting equipment, makes it easier to address problems related to lack of orientation and balance.
No control is required, and the handle provides security in case of loss of balance.
The beginner is closer to the horse when there is no saddle; in motion, he first learns to manage his shoulders and torso.
Lunge work can also be done with a saddle and a neck strap to hold on to in case of imbalance.

With small, reliable, and gentle ponies, games can be organized with groups of children who freely discover the needs imposed by control.
This method has the great advantage of reducing or eliminating initial anxieties while quickly introducing the issues related to speed and control.
This is possible by directing the child’s attention to the game, its rules, and the peer group that must carry it out.

Every teaching method will be applied correctly in relation to the instructor’s preparation as well as the operational context.
The adoption of different methods is still aimed at the same solution of problems and the same goal: to teach riding correctly, while seeking to engage and retain practitioners.

Initially, the distance from the ground, the absence of a solid support surface for the feet, and the horse’s movement lead the beginner to use gripping with the hands to overcome imbalance.
From the first months of life, humans tend to reestablish balance with their hands.
When they begin to walk, they establish balance on a support plane, which is the ground.

These two essential elements of human balance can make it difficult to learn equestrian balance.
For a horse, carrying a rider’s weight is an effort that becomes acceptable if the weight is active and adapts to the movement, but tiring and unacceptable if the weight is passive and resists movement.
The only condition to make the rider’s weight acceptable to the horse is that the rider is in balance and able to maintain balance during the horse’s movement.
This is the first principle of equestrian technique.

Even in mounting the horse — whether the rider mounts in support (jumping with the abdomen on the withers and swinging one leg over to sit astride), mounts with the help of the stirrup (placing the foot in the stirrup without disturbing the ribs and holding onto the withers), or mounts with help from the ground — he must always take care to place his weight on the horse’s back progressively and as delicately as possible, first distributing it through the stirrups and then through the seat.

8.1 Gymnastics on Horseback

The gymnastics required of a rider must first aim at elasticity and coordination of movements, then at strengthening certain muscular structures.

Strengthening of the back and abdominal muscles.
In equestrian sports, strengthening the back muscles supports the spine, gives compactness to the torso, protects from microtraumas, and allows torso movements.

Strengthening of the leg muscles.
In riding, more than the extensors or flexors, it is the adductors that must be strengthened.
In any case, excessive increase in muscle mass volume reduces flexibility and quickness in its use, and therefore should not be pursued.

Exercises aimed at stimulating balance.
With a horse held on the lunge, mount in a kneeling position on its back, first holding a handle, then standing.
Then perform the same exercise at walk, possibly at trot and canter.

With a horse held on the lunge, in a seated position, follow the horse through all three gaits: first holding onto a handle, then with hands on hips, folded, behind the back, clapping hands to the rhythm of the canter.

With a horse on the lunge, perform the “around the world”: swinging one leg over at a time, sit on the back in four positions — front, rear, and the two lateral — first with the horse standing, then at walk, and eventually at trot and canter.

With a saddled horse on the lunge, rise above the saddle supported only by the stirrups, first at halt, then at walk, trot, and finally canter, maintaining the position even during transitions between gaits.

Exercises that stimulate orientation.
On a horse held on the lunge, keep the eyes closed.
Within the circle, a designated point must be recognized.
The exercise can be performed at all three gaits.

CHAPTER 9: Position and Seat

9.1 Position
The position is the way the rider arranges the parts of his body while on horseback.
Depending on the horse’s dynamic needs, and therefore on stirrup length, three main positions can be identified: seated, raised, and racing position.
The position serves the search for balance and harmony.

A premature search for position, before a sense of confidence has been established, produces unnecessary stiffness.
The position is constantly refined.
From a position functional to the search for balance, one will achieve a position of minimal effort and maximum use of the aids.
The first is essential in the search for the seat, while the second is integrated with a good perception of balance and a high degree of sensitivity.

The position must therefore be constantly perfected, and like sensitivity and seat, the result can only be obtained with long practice, reflective experience made of critique and self-critique, much observation, and many different horses ridden.

The rider sits astride the horse with the legs.
This astride position constitutes the largest surface in contact with the horse.

The correct stirrup length plays a central role in the search for proper position.
At the beginning, a single stirrup length will be used, but soon one must adopt a longer stirrup length for flatwork, and a shorter one for jumping.
There are no fixed parameters for applying the correct stirrup length.
It must be chosen for each individual rider, respecting their proportions in relation to the length of their legs and torso, considering muscular availability (ability to adapt and stretch), the horse’s morphology, the type of saddle, and its specific use.

Stirrup length must allow a comfortable placement of the leg under the pelvis, keeping the joint angles of ankle, knee, and hip well defined.
In general, one can follow the guidelines that there should be four fingers between the pommel and the rider’s groin when in the raised position, or that, with the leg extended, the tip of the foot, when lifted, must be able to reach the stirrup.
These are indicative parameters, not binding rules.

It is very important that the stirrup leathers are even, with equal length, so that the weight can be distributed equally between right and left.

The foot must rest solidly on the stirrup, which should be perpendicular to the horse’s side, and “worn” with the ball of the foot.
In some cases, with short stirrups and the need for greater stability, the stirrup can be worn up to the heel of the boot.
A support on the stirrup obtained with the tip of the foot must be avoided, as it tends to push the leg forward and makes the support unstable.

The head must be high and free, the shoulders open and mobile, without stiffness, the torso toned and compact.
The arms must be close to the torso.
The elbows close to the torso both in seated and raised positions, and they may be slightly more forward in the raised position.
The forearms must be directed towards the horse’s mouth so as to align with the reins.

The elbow angle must be well defined: this is the only way the hands can follow the horse’s mouth without involving the torso in movement.
This position, essential for the search for contact, is extremely important for respecting the horse’s mouth, a highly sensitive part.

The hands must hold the reins so that they enter between the ring finger and little finger and exit from the fist, secured by the thumb.
The hands must close on the reins without stiffness and must not be open.
The wrists must be relaxed and not bent.
The fists should be mostly vertical.
Poor hand position will make it difficult to establish proper contact.

9.2 Seat
The seat is the ability to adapt positions to the horse’s movement so that there is constant balance.
The seat is measured by the relationship between the horse’s center of gravity and that of the rider — a relationship that allows unity between the two and the creation of a partnership.
The seat is an indispensable quality for communicating with the horse.

The absence of seat prevents the rider from using the legs to stay in the saddle and does not allow communication with the hands, which are constantly moving due to torso oscillations.

The saddle size must allow the thighs to fit comfortably into the flaps, without the pommel restricting movement.
A stirrup length that is too short makes it more difficult to find balance in the raised position, pushes the torso too far forward, and reduces the pelvis’s ability to follow the horse’s movement in the seated position.
A stirrup length that is too long encourages the leg to slide forward, lose the stirrups, and consequently lose balance.

Since balance cannot be achieved unless weight is distributed on a base of support perpendicular to the ground, it is therefore necessary that the stirrup on which the foot rests is under the rider’s pelvis.

9.3 The Seat in the Seated Position
This position allows the rider to accommodate the many shifts of center of gravity in flatwork, which often require repositioning the center of gravity backwards relative to the mass, thus lightening the horse’s forehand.

The deep astride position, allowed by longer stirrup length, and the contact of the buttocks, enables greater communication with the horse through the actions of the legs and weight of the body.

In the seated position, the head, shoulders, hips, and point of foot contact on the stirrup should all align along a line perpendicular to the ground.
In assuming this position, the torso stretches upward, the legs extend downward, the head is high and free, the buttocks in contact with the saddle as far forward as possible.

The thighs and legs are close to the flaps and to the horse.
The feet are generally parallel to the horse, resting on the stirrup at the widest point of the sole.
As the knee descends, the heel lowers without meeting resistance in the ankle.

The torso must be vertical to ensure proper weight distribution on the horse, but must also stretch vertically to activate the supporting muscles of the spine and reduce vertebral curves as much as possible.

The high head facilitates correct position, but for this active stretching to benefit the lumbar region, the pelvis’s disposition is fundamental.

The pelvis in anterior tilt, with pressure on the pubis and buttocks back, puts the lumbar spine in hyperlordosis and prevents adaptation to the horse’s movement.
Since the horse’s back moves vertically and forward, such pelvic placement prevents absorption of vertical motion and clashes with forward movement.

By contrast, a pelvis in posterior tilt, with buttocks in contact with the saddle and relaxed lower back, allows adaptation to the horse’s longitudinal movement, absorbs vertical motion, and reduces lumbar lordosis.

When working without stirrups, it is not necessary for the heel to be lowered.
Without the stirrup, lowering the heel requires ankle activation to lift the toe, which often affects the knee and in any case activates a part that should remain passive.

The feet remain parallel to the horse or slightly apart.
Outward rotation of the toes causes the knee to move away from the saddle’s flaps, compromising the necessary adhesion.

9.4 The Seat in the Raised Position
This position allows the rider to adapt to a wide range of dynamic situations.
It grants the horse’s back total freedom, which it requires in jumping and fast cantering, allows the torso to adapt to major changes of center of gravity, and, by bringing the buttocks closer to the saddle, permits adjustment to a rearward shift of gravity and good communication with the horse.

The rider’s body parts align along a line perpendicular to the ground that passes through the head, shoulders, knee, and toe.
The stirrup length is shorter than the one used to favor the seated position.

In the raised position, a deep astride placement must be sought to avoid instability that pushes the torso too far forward, lifts the heels, and brings the pelvis onto the pommel.

The opposing angles of ankle and knee contribute to making the stirrup support solid and steady.
The knee must descend as far down as possible because it increases the contact surface with the horse.
Upon meeting the stirrup, the passive ankle lets the heel drop.
The knee, ready to descend forward and downward, accompanies the horse’s forward movement, keeping the two centers of gravity — horse and rider — together at all times.

The shoulders must remain open even in the raised position, but they must not lose mobility.

The transition from seated to raised position, and vice versa, must occur through the action of the joint angles, avoiding that body segments move too far away from the line perpendicular to the ground passing through the pelvis.
