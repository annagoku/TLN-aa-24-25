CHAPTER 4: Hygiene and Prevention

4.1 The Stable
Often, the horse is forced to spend long hours of its day confined in a stable; the box in which it lives is therefore a very important place and must have characteristics of safety and comfort.
First of all, space: the larger, the better. In any case, it should not be smaller than 3 meters by 3 meters, preferably 3.50 meters by 3.50 meters.
Some horses, especially younger ones unfamiliar with this unnatural home, may assume positions when lying down that make it difficult for them to get back up due to the box walls. A larger area or the addition of a raised bedding along the sides or a wall where they can push against helps prevent this dangerous issue.
The "bowls"—automatic water dispensers—should be placed at a suitable height, easily accessible to the horse, but not too low, and preferably near a corner so they are not in the way should the horse roll while lying down.
The manger should also be placed in a corner, constructed without sharp edges and low enough for the horse to eat in a natural position, without food contamination.
Bowls and mangers must be kept clean.
It is important that the box is not a dusty place because inhaling dust over long periods can cause respiratory issues or trigger allergic reactions. The stable must be well-ventilated with high ceilings, while avoiding drafts that could hit the horse directly and risk bronchitis.
The true enemy of the athletic horse is boredom.
For this reason, stables built so that horses can see each other and look outside are the best.
In any case, if doors do not have an upper grate, they should be reasonably high so that the horse cannot lean on them with its chest, which can cause abrasions.
There are numerous ways to distract a bored horse or one affected by vices such as “weaving” or “cribbing,” but the best remedy remains movement.
The materials used for bedding are quite varied, ranging from straw to wood shavings, rice husks, and even processed newspaper.
Each material has its advantages and disadvantages and requires different care.
In all cases, the bedding must be soft, dry, as clean as possible, and must not force the standing horse into unnatural positions, which can happen if the bedding is uneven.
Periodically, it must be entirely removed, and the floor as well as the walls must be disinfected, as should the corridors be disinfected frequently.
It should be remembered that the majority of hoof diseases derive from poor stable hygiene.
Horses can also be stabled in tie stalls measuring 3 meters in length and no less than 1.70 meters in width.
This type of stabling considerably reduces the horse’s ability to move and imposes some safety standards to allow the horse to lie down.
It requires the horse to always wear a halter attached to a rope that passes through a ring, at the other end of which is a counterweight that acts as a stopper being larger than the ring.
The practicality of this type of housing comes entirely at the expense of the horse’s comfort and well-being.

4.2 Feeding
The horse has adapted to a very different dietary regime than its natural one.
The first thing to consider is the relationship between fuel food—energy value, the quantity of feed—and consumption, that is, the athletic effort the horse is subjected to.
Equally important is the obvious relationship between the quantity of food and the weight of the horse.
The choice of feed and how to distribute it throughout the day must consider how the horse digests.
Salivary glands attached to the mouth begin the digestive process through enzymes.
From the pharynx begins a cylindrical canal called the esophagus, which leads to the stomach.
The stomach holds about 15 liters on average, small compared to the size of the animal; it must fill and empty before the ration is fully consumed.
The stomach connects the esophagus to the small intestine, which is linked to two large glands: the liver and the pancreas. The small intestine leads to the large intestine, subdivided into cecum, colon, and rectum.
The cecum is very large and important because it serves as a reservoir for liquid feed, which the horse greatly needs.
The daily food quantity required must be divided into as many portions as possible, compatible with the stable’s management, bearing in mind that the initial digestive process lasts at least 90 minutes during which the horse should not be subjected to exertion that would reduce the blood flow needed for digestion.
The feeds commonly given to horses are many.
To provide only some general and therefore generic guidelines, one can say that a horse weighing about 500 kg in moderate work can be maintained with about 5 kg of cereals (barley and oats, for example) and 8 kg of hay per day.
Barley and oats can be fed as whole grains, crushed, or flaked.
Whole grains retain all nutrients, unlike crushed or flaked, but some unmasticated grains can be found undigested in the feces.
Wheat and corn, although high in nutrients, are not recommended as staple feeds for horses because they are poorly digestible but can be used as energy supplements based on the horse’s workload.
Vegetable oils are also used to increase fat content in the horse’s diet.
Hay must be low in dust, free of mold, and sourced from uncontaminated fields.
There are many types of grasses and legumes used for hay, each with different properties.
Generally, first-cut hay is preferred; that which is usually harvested and dried in May, known as “maggengo,” is among the richest in minerals and fiber.
Bran, when mixed with crushed grains cooked in ample water, in moderate amounts, is beneficial because it facilitates bowel emptying.
A periodic mash aids digestive processes, but the composition and frequency of mash feeding must be regulated by experienced personnel to avoid intestinal issues.
Supplementing dry feed with carrots and beets is pleasant for the horse and encourages appetite.
Transitioning from a dry to a fresh green diet (fresh pasture) must be done gradually to prevent digestive problems.
Water is by far the most important nutrient; it must not be too cold or contaminated. The most practical solution is an automatic watering system.
If watering manually, it should be offered frequently throughout the day, before feeding to avoid interfering with digestion, providing 20–30 liters of water depending on the climate.
In any case, avoid letting the horse drink cold water after work while it is still hot.
When feeding the horse, never forget to provide salt; the horse will regulate its intake according to its needs.

4.3 Grooming
The horse’s body must be cleaned regularly.
In nature, horses roll, scratch, and groom each other.
The massage effect of the curry comb and stiff brush not only meets hygiene needs but is also pleasant and stimulating for the horse’s muscles.
It is also a good way to build trust, as the horse gets used to the human’s scent and touch.
The limbs, muzzle, and sensitive areas must be brushed gently; neck, back, croup, and ribs can be massaged with the curry comb and brush.
The hooves must be cleaned before and after work to remove stones and debris from the frog and sole, and to remove manure and urine that, when packed into the frog, can cause thrush.
To retain moisture in the hoof wall and maintain hoof elasticity, the hoof must be greased periodically.
The tail should be washed often and brushed gently and infrequently to avoid hair loss. The mane should be thinned so as not to interfere with the reins and to facilitate braiding.
A cloth is used for the final cleaning, and a sponge for delicate areas.
In winter, it is advisable to protect horses with blankets to shield them from cold and dampness; blanketing is necessary for clipped horses.
Partial or complete clipping of the long winter coat is done in autumn (October or November, depending on the climate) to prevent horses in work from sweating excessively and then cooling down too slowly.
Showers and baths in summer support thermoregulation and help maintain coat cleanliness. Drying horses is not always easy; it is best to scrape off water with a sweat scraper and then walk the horse.
When trimming excess hair, one must consider the protective function that hair and mane have over the areas they cover.
The forelock should not be overly shortened, the hairs protruding from the ear should only be trimmed slightly, and the tail hair can be cut to the level of the hock.

4.4 Approaching Horses
The horse is not aggressive towards humans; if it appears so, it is likely due to abuse or a fearful nature.
Respecting safety rules and building mutual trust are key to approaching a horse calmly.
Giving the horse time to understand who it is dealing with is a good rule: let it smell the person, see who is approaching, and ensure there are no sudden, high movements or hesitant actions that could be misinterpreted. The horse should not experience unnecessary pain or restraints.

4.5 Dental Prevention
Regular dental checks are important, as uneven wear on molars can cause painful sharp edges which, over time, create serious problems with chewing and digestion.
These edges may injure the inside of the cheeks and cause discomfort with the bit.
A six-month dental check-up by a vet will allow, if necessary, to smooth out sharp edges and remove tartar, keeping the entire digestive system efficient.

4.6 Deworming
Deworming is necessary to prevent and treat infestations of small parasitic worms found in plant-based food, especially in pastures, where their population is constantly increased by manure containing eggs and larvae.

4.7 Tetanus Vaccine
The tetanus bacillus is found in horse feces, and its spores are extremely resistant, reproducing only in anaerobic conditions.
It becomes very dangerous when it enters a wound and finds the conditions to multiply.
It produces a powerful toxin that spreads through the nervous system and can lead to death.
The vaccine must be administered annually, with the first dose followed by a booster after one month.
In case of a serious wound, immediately administer the serum and ensure the wound is well disinfected and oxygenated.

4.8 Influenza Vaccine
To combat the most dangerous strains of influenza viruses, there are vaccines that must be administered on a regular annual basis.

4.9 Shoeing
Shoeing is not necessary for wild horses but is very important for sport horses. It protects the hoof when working on varied terrain, improves traction through studs, and allows correction of conformation and gait issues via trimming (hoof cutting).
The choice of shoeing method, and more importantly trimming, is crucial for proper athletic performance.
Shoeing can be done cold, where the farrier shapes the shoe after trimming. In hot shoeing, the shoe is tried on while hot, helping it fit perfectly to the hoof.
This method results in a more secure fit and better distribution of pressure, but care must be taken not to burn or inflame the sensitive parts of the hoof.
Shoeing can correct defects in hoof proportion, shape, and wall quality.
The farrier will adjust the trim and shape of the shoe to encourage healthy hoof growth.
A hoof that is too small and contracted requires wide-webbed shoes, heels should not be trimmed, and the frog must be preserved.
A flat hoof needs the sole preserved and a covered, beveled shoe.
A soft hoof demands careful trimming, while a dry hoof is better with a shoe with few nail holes to avoid cracks.
In some hoof pathologies causing deformation, proper shoeing can relieve pain.
All shoeing that alters the hoof’s shape is called corrective shoeing.
Shoes are usually replaced every forty days, but growth rate and trimming needs vary per horse.

4.10 Pathologies
The main locomotor disorders show up as lameness or localized swelling, often at the site of injury; rest and cold water applications are advisable while waiting for the vet.
Creams or stable wraps should only be used with veterinary approval.
Lameness usually signals pain, but there can also be mechanical lameness due to joint stiffness or muscle contractions without pain.
Bleeding wounds require immediate veterinary intervention.
Meanwhile, the wound can be rinsed with cold water, disinfected, and bandaged.
If bleeding is rhythmic with heartbeats, it is arterial and very serious—apply a tourniquet upstream if possible.
Digestive system disorders also need quick veterinary care.
Colic causes intense pain shown by pawing the ground, looking at the flank, lying down, or pressing the abdomen.
The horse must be fasted, kept calm (especially if lying down), kept warm, and possibly given antispasmodics or painkillers.
Respiratory dysfunction may show as coughing, nasal discharge, or labored breathing; avoid dust and temperature swings and await the vet.
Daily skin checks allow early intervention in skin diseases and allergic reactions.
At the first sign of hives, blisters, or abnormal growths, consult the vet. Fungal infections are contagious.
Careful hoof inspection helps detect thrush, cracks, and white line disease early.
